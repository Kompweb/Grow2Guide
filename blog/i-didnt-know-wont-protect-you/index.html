Nobody explained the policies at orientation — but let’s be honest, you didn’t read them either

By Sara Tudor · October 6, 2026 · 4 min read

In this article
  1. Two versions of the same story
  2. Why “I didn’t know” doesn’t protect you
  3. What actually fixes this
  4. Three things you can do this week
  5. The orientation I wish I’d been handed

Remember orientation? They sat you down with a binder — or worse, a link to a folder of PDFs — and said, “Read through these and initial each one.”

You initialed. All of them. Maybe you skimmed the first three. By page forty, you were just signing to get it over with.

Then a month later, something happens on shift. You handle it the way that made sense to you. And your supervisor says, “That’s against policy.”

And you think: what policy? Nobody explained any of this to me.

Two versions of the same story

Here’s the uncomfortable part. There are two versions of this story, and they both happen every single day.

Version one: you actually read them. You had questions — real ones, about situations you could already see coming on your shift. You asked. Nobody had answers, or nobody had time. Now you’re expected to follow policies that nobody could explain to you. That’s an agency problem, and it’s a fair complaint.

Version two: you didn’t read a single one. You initialed your way through the stack on day one because everyone does, because HR was waiting, because you just wanted to get to the actual job. Then something goes wrong and you say, “Nobody explained this to me.” And somewhere, a supervisor is thinking: you never opened the binder. I’m not going to sit here and walk you through 120 policies you didn’t read.

Both of these are true. Sometimes at the same agency, in the same week.

Here’s what I know from being on both sides of this: the system sets everyone up. Agencies hand you a mountain of paperwork because it covers them — you signed it, so you’re accountable. New hires initial their way through it because it’s overwhelming and nobody makes it digestible. Then a policy gets violated, and everyone acts surprised.

Why “I didn’t know” doesn’t protect you

And here’s the part nobody says out loud: “I didn’t know” doesn’t protect you. You signed it. Whether you read it or not, whether someone explained it or not — your initials are on every page. That’s the part that follows you into a write-up, an investigation, a lost job.

I’ve watched good employees — people who genuinely cared about this work — get written up over policies they signed but never understood. Not because they were careless. Because nobody ever made it make sense to them. And there’s something awful about sitting across from someone being disciplined for something they didn’t know, thinking: this didn’t have to happen.

That’s the part that stays with me. It was never that people didn’t care enough to learn. Nobody made it learnable.

What actually fixes this

Not a longer orientation. Not someone reading policies aloud to you.

What fixes it is understanding the why. Every policy exists to protect someone — the client, you, your team, or the agency. When you know who a policy is protecting, you don’t have to memorize it. You can figure out the right move even in a situation the binder never mentioned.

A behavioral health technician who understands why incident reports have deadlines doesn’t wait until tomorrow to write one. A mental health technician who understands why confidentiality matters doesn’t tell a caller “she’s doing fine.” That’s not memorization. That’s judgment — and it’s what supervisors notice.

Three things you can do this week

You don’t need to read all 120 policies tonight. Start here:

  1. Pick the three policies most likely to come up on your shift: documentation, incident reporting, and confidentiality. Read just those, slowly.
  2. For each one, ask your supervisor one question: “What does this look like on my shift?” Write the answer down.
  3. Find out your deadlines. When is documentation due? When is an incident report due? Who do you call first? A lot of write-ups start with a deadline someone never knew existed.
