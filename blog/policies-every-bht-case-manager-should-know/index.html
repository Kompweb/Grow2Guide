Policies Every BHT and Case Manager Should Know

By Grow2Guide · September 25, 2026

Most policy manuals feel like they were written for someone else. Yet as a behavioral health technician (BHT) or case manager, you are the person most likely to meet these policies in real time: at intake, during a crisis, while writing a note at the end of a long shift. Knowing the main policy areas makes those moments less stressful.

Every organization writes its own policies, and state and federal rules add to them. Treat this article as a map, not a rulebook. Your employer’s manual and your supervisor have the final word.

The policy areas that touch your day

1. Client rights and confidentiality

Who may see client information, what you may share, and with whom. Privacy laws such as HIPAA shape this in many settings, and substance use records can carry extra protections. When you are unsure, ask before you share.

2. Documentation

What must be recorded, how soon, and in what format. Notes show that care happened and let the next person pick up where you left off.

3. Incident and critical event reporting

What counts as an incident, who you tell, how quickly, and what you write down. Small events are worth knowing how to report, not only serious ones.

4. Abuse, neglect and mandatory reporting

Many people in these roles have reporting duties, and the details vary by state and by role. Find out exactly what applies to you and who at your organization to contact.

5. Crisis, safety and emergency procedures

What to do when someone is in crisis, when there is a safety concern, or when the building needs to be evacuated. Know the steps before you need them.

6. Professional boundaries and ethics

Gifts, dual relationships, social media, and contact outside work hours. Boundaries protect clients and you.

7. The scope of your role

What you may do, and what belongs to a licensed clinician, a nurse or a supervisor. If a task is not clearly part of your role, ask first.

8. Complaints and grievances

How a client or family member raises a concern, and what you do when they do.

Three questions to find which policies are yours

  1. Who is named? Look for your job title or a description of your duties.
  2. What does it ask me to do, or not do? Underline the action words.
  3. Why does it exist? Knowing the reason makes the rule easier to follow and to explain.

Once you can answer those three for each area above, you already understand more of your manual than you did when you started. For a method you can use on any policy, read How to Read a Policy Manual Without Getting Lost.

This article is educational and not legal advice. Follow your organization’s own policies and your state’s requirements.
