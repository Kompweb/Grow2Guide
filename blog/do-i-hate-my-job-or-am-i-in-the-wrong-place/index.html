Do I Hate My Job, or Am I Just in the Wrong Place?

By Grow2Guide · September 25, 2026

Someone asks what you do for a living.

“I work with kids,” you say.

“Aww, that’s awesome!” they say. “That must be so rewarding.”

And you smile and nod, because what are you supposed to say? Not really. Most days I dread going in. I count the hours. I fantasize about quitting in the parking lot.

You don’t say that part out loud. You carry it home with you, and it sits there, heavy, while you wonder what’s wrong with you. Everyone else seems to love this work. The mission matters. The kids need you. So why do you feel like this?

Here’s a question worth sitting with: do you actually hate this work, or are you just in the wrong place?

They feel the same at 6 a.m. when your alarm goes off. But they are not the same thing.

Hating the work vs. being in the wrong place

Hating the work

The population doesn’t move you. The day-to-day drains you even on the good days. You’ve tried different roles and different agencies, and the feeling follows you. It’s not the supervisor, it’s not the shift, it’s not the paperwork. It’s the work itself, and somewhere in you, you’ve known that for a while.

Being in the wrong place

You care about the work, but everything around it is breaking you. The agency is chaotic. Leadership is absent or toxic. You’re understaffed every single shift. The policies exist on paper, but nobody follows them. You go home angry at the system, not the kids.

Most people I talk to are in the second group. They don’t hate the work. They hate where they’re doing it.

Why the agency matters as much as the job title

Here’s what nobody tells you when you’re new: two behavioral health technicians (BHTs) can have the same role, the same population and the same pay, and one is thriving while the other is barely surviving. The difference is very often the organization around them: clear expectations, real supervision, policies that actually get followed, and leadership that backs you up when a shift goes sideways.

When you’re new to this field, you don’t know what a healthy agency looks like yet. So the chaos starts to feel normal, and the dread starts to feel like just part of the job. It isn’t.

Four questions worth asking yourself

I’ve watched a lot of good people leave this field over the wrong problem. If any of this sounds familiar, ask yourself:

  • On your best days there, does the work itself still matter to you?
  • Are you exhausted by the kids, or exhausted by the adults running the place?
  • If you did this same job at a well-run agency, do you think you’d feel different?
  • Have you ever actually seen what a functional program looks like?

If what you’re feeling points more to the place than to the work itself, that’s worth knowing. It changes what you do next.

Nobody teaches you how to read an agency

You’re just supposed to know what good supervision looks like, which policies actually protect you, and the difference between a program that’s struggling and one that’s just badly run.

That’s why I wrote the Behavioral Health Policy Inventory & Learning Guide, so people new to this field don’t have to learn it the hard way, one bad job at a time. It’s a 69-topic policy inventory built around one question: “What does it have to do with me?” It’s the stuff I wish someone had handed me before my first shift.

You don’t have to love where you are right now. But you get to choose where you go next, and it’s a lot easier to choose well when you know what you’re looking at.

There’s nothing wrong with leaving a bad fit. The trick is knowing what you’re actually leaving.
