“Client was aggressive” is not a note

By Sara Tudor · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

In this article
  1. Labels vs. observations
  2. Why it matters more than you think
  3. Five labels, rewritten
  4. Don’t forget the other half: you
  5. A quick checklist before you hit submit
  6. The only question that matters

Every supervisor has read this line a hundred times:

“Client was aggressive.”

Sometimes it’s the whole note. Sometimes it’s followed by “Staff redirected.” And that’s it. Shift over.

Here’s the problem: that note tells the next person almost nothing. Aggressive how? Toward whom? What happened right before? What did staff do? Did it work? Is anyone hurt? Should the next shift be worried?

The person who wrote it knows exactly what happened. Nobody else does. And in a week, the person who wrote it won’t either.

I once read a note that said: “Client was hard to be redirected and did not follow direction.”

That was it. And the staff member who wrote it was off for the next three days.

So the supervisor came in the next morning, read the note, and went to check in with the client — just to know where to start. He couldn’t simply text the staff member to ask what happened. Contacting an hourly employee on their day off can turn into paid work time, and that’s a whole other conversation.

The resident told him: “I didn’t want to eat, so I did my chore and my hygiene, and went to bed early.”

Now nothing made sense. Hard to redirect from what? What direction wasn’t followed — dinner? Was skipping a meal the concern, or was it something else entirely? The note had a conclusion and no story. And the only person who knew the story wasn’t there to tell it.

Labels vs. observations

Most weak documentation has the same problem: it describes a conclusion instead of what happened.

“Aggressive” is a conclusion. So are “manipulative,” “attention-seeking,” “noncompliant,” and “had a good day.” They’re your interpretation of what you saw — and two staff members can watch the exact same moment and come to two different conclusions.

Nobody can argue with what was actually observed.

A label tells the reader what you think. An observation tells them what happened — and lets clinicians, nurses, supervisors, and the next shift draw their own conclusions from the facts.

Why it matters more than you think

Your notes aren’t just paperwork. They’re read by:

  • The next shift, walking in blind without them
  • Clinicians, making treatment decisions based on what you wrote
  • Supervisors and QA, reviewing whether services happened as required
  • Auditors and licensing reviewers, checking the record
  • Sometimes attorneys — and sometimes the client and their family

And here’s the part people forget: your note is also your record. When questions come up later, your documentation speaks for you. A vague note can’t show what you did right.

Five labels, rewritten

Your agency’s format might be SOAP, DAP, BIRP, or its own template. The container changes. What goes inside it shouldn’t.

1. “Aggressive”

Weak noteClient was aggressive.

Strong noteAt 3:15 p.m., client threw a plastic chair toward the wall approximately five feet from peers and stated, “Leave me alone.”

2. “Manipulative”

Weak noteClient was manipulating staff to get out of chores.

Strong noteWhen asked to complete an assigned chore, client told staff that another staff member had said they did not have to do chores today. Staff confirmed with the shift lead that the chore was assigned. Client completed the chore with one additional prompt.

3. “Attention-seeking”

Weak noteClient was attention-seeking during group.

Strong noteDuring group, client interrupted peers four times and laughed loudly while others were sharing. Staff redirected client twice. Client participated appropriately during the final fifteen minutes.

4. “Noncompliant”

Weak noteClient was noncompliant with the rules.

Strong noteClient did not return to the unit at the end of free time after two verbal reminders. Client returned at 7:20 p.m., twenty minutes after the scheduled time, after staff offered a choice of two evening activities.

5. “Had a good day”

Weak noteClient had a good day.

Strong noteClient attended all three scheduled groups, participated verbally in two, and completed assigned tasks without additional prompting.

Notice that even the “good day” needed fixing. Vague positive notes are just as useless as vague negative ones. If someone is making progress, the record should be able to prove it.

Don’t forget the other half: you

A note that only describes the client’s behavior tells half the story. What did you do, and how did they respond?

Weak noteClient upset. Calmed down.

Strong noteClient was pacing and raising their voice. Staff offered a quiet space and two coping options. Client chose to listen to music in the common area. After approximately ten minutes, client rejoined the group and spoke at a normal volume.

That second note shows the next shift what works. It shows the clinician the client used a coping skill. And it shows anyone reviewing it that you did your job well.

A quick checklist before you hit submit

  • Who, what, when, where, and how much? Times, numbers, and specifics beat adjectives.
  • Did I quote what they said, instead of summarizing how they said it?
  • Did I describe my actions and how they responded?
  • Did I only document what I personally saw or did? If it came from someone else, say so: “Per day-shift staff, client did not eat lunch.”
  • Is it on time? Memory fades fast, especially after a long shift.

The only question that matters

Before you submit any note, ask yourself:

“If I weren’t here tomorrow, could someone read this and know exactly what happened — and what to do next?”

If the answer is yes, you did your job.

If the answer is no, take two more minutes. Those two minutes may matter more than you’ll ever know.
